# OLD YELLOW TOP — CHARACTER RESEARCH FOUNDATION

**Subject:** "Old Yellow Top" / "Yellow Top" / "Pre-Cambrian Shield Man" — a reported hairy bipedal figure associated with the silver-mining district around Cobalt, Ontario, Canada.
**Compiled:** 19 September 2026
**Purpose:** Authoritative research substrate for a knowledge-corpus / character-interview system. Not a biography. Not an argument for or against existence.
**Status of document:** Complete as a synthesis of what is publicly retrievable online as of this date. Archival work not yet performed (see §15.4, Research Gaps).

### 0.3 A note on quotation

Witness wording is central to this case, but the surviving wording exists only inside modern transcriptions and copyrighted secondary works. **This document paraphrases rather than reproduces.** Where a phrase is given in quotation marks it is a short attributed fragment; everything else is paraphrase, even where it closely tracks a witness's sense. **Any downstream system that needs the exact testimonial wording must go to the transcriptions and, better, to the microfilm** (§15.1). Do not let a paraphrase in this document be promoted to "what the witness said."

### 0.4 The single most important structural fact

**No original newspaper page for any of the four events has been examined for this compilation.** Everything below rests on:

1. a set of clippings held by Canadian researcher **John Green**, said to have reached him from **René Dahinden**;
2. Green's publication of them in his own books;
3. a web transcription of those clippings (bigfootencounters.com, credited "courtesy John Green … 1997"), mirrored on sasquatchcanada.com;
4. later authors quoting Green.

That is a **single evidentiary channel**. Every "1923 article said…" statement in this document is really "a transcription attributed to Green's clipping of the 1923 article says…". This is the case's central provenance weakness and it must be carried forward into the corpus, not smoothed away.

## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

`DOCUMENTED REPORT`

Between 1923 and 1970 the North Bay *Nugget* — the regional daily for the Temiskaming district of northeastern Ontario, founded in 1903-boom Cobalt itself — carried at least three short items about a dark, hair-covered, upright-walking figure with a light or yellow head, reported in the bush around the Cobalt silver camp. Those items, and one retrospective reference inside the earliest of them, generate the four "sightings" that constitute the entire case:

| # | Date of event | Place | Witnesses | Publication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | September 1906 (reported) | Violet Mine, east of Cobalt | unnamed headframe construction crew | **none contemporaneous known**; first appears 1923 |
| 2 | July 1923 | claims N/NE of the Wettlaufer Mine, South Lorrain Twp. | J. A. MacAuley and (Lorne/Lorno) Wilson, prospectors | North Bay *Nugget*, 27 July 1923 |
| 3 | April 1946 **or** April 1947 | near Gillies Lake / Gillies Depot, Coleman Twp. | an unnamed woman and her son | North Bay *Nugget*, 16 April 1946 or 1947 |
| 4 | evening of 4 August 1970 | gravel road to the Cobalt Lode Mine, SW of Cobalt | bus driver (Aimee/Amos) Latreille; miner Larry Cormack; ~27 miners aboard | North Bay *Nugget*, 5 August 1970 |

**What is solidly established:** that these reports exist; that the nickname "Old Yellow-Top" was already in newspaper use by July 1923 and was explained there as deriving from a light-coloured mane; that the newspaper also used the phrase "Pre-Cambrian Shield Man"; that the physical settings — the Violet Mine, the Wettlaufer Mine, Gillies Depot, Gillies Lake, the Cobalt Lode Mine, the T&NO railway, the bush roads — are all real, datable, mappable places whose histories are independently documented.

**What is not established:** the existence of any animal; that the four reports describe one individual; any height, weight, age, sex, odour, vocal repertoire beyond a single reported growl, footprint, photograph, or physical trace whatsoever. **There is no physical evidence in this case of any kind.** No tracks were reported in any of the four accounts. No photograph with a traceable provenance exists.

**What the most serious naturalistic reading offers:** an upright-standing American black bear, possibly with an anomalous light patch or sun-bleached crown, seen at distance, in low light, or in headlights, by observers who in three of the four cases explicitly said "bear" first. This is the explanation advanced by skeptic Joe Nickell, who calls the Cobalt figure "a blond-maned black bear." It is unfalsifiable in either direction on the surviving evidence.

**What is folkloric accretion:** the seven-foot height, the 400-pound weight, glowing or red eyes, a foul odour, a limp, footprints, a purported 1923 photograph, and the continuous-observation-throughout-construction version of 1906. None of these appear in the newspaper transcriptions. All are quarantined in §16.

### 2.1 Was "Old Yellow Top" the original name, or a later popularisation?

`SOURCE FACT` — **It was already in circulation in 1923.** The 1923 *Nugget* item itself states that the creature was *nicknamed* "Old Yellow-Top" on account of a light-coloured mane, and frames the 1923 event as the second sighting in seventeen years. The nickname is therefore attested in print at least 47 years before the 1970 event.

`UNRESOLVED` — Whether the nickname existed **in 1906** cannot be determined. The 1923 article presents the name as already established local usage but does not date its coinage. Two readings are equally available from the text: (a) the name grew up around the district after 1906 and the 1923 reporter simply used it; (b) the reporter coined or firmed it up in 1923 while writing up the new report and back-projected it onto 1906. **The corpus must not assert that the 1906 workers used the name.**

`SECONDARY INTERPRETATION` — "Pre-Cambrian Shield Man" reads as a reporter's flourish rather than vernacular. It appears in headline position in 1923 and again in 1970, suggesting either an in-house *Nugget* convention or a 1970 writer reaching back to the paper's own morgue. There is no evidence anyone in Cobalt said it aloud.

### 2.2 Name-form variants to normalise on ingestion

- Old Yellow Top ≡ Old Yellow-Top ≡ Yellow Top ≡ Old Yellowtop ≡ "old yellowtop"
- Pre-Cambrian Shield Man ≡ PreCambrian Shield Man ≡ Precambrian Shield Man
- Witness surnames: MacAuley ≡ MacAulay ≡ Macaulay ≡ McAuley (unresolved; see §14)
- Wilson given name: Lorne ≡ Lorno (unresolved)
- 1970 driver: Latreille ≡ Latrielle ≡ Latrille; given name Aimee ≡ Aimé ≡ Amos (unresolved)

## 3. GEOGRAPHIC FOUNDATION

`SOURCE FACT`

### 3.1 Core coordinates and relations

- **Cobalt, Ontario** — 47°24′N, 79°41′W; elevation ~295 m; Timiskaming District, northeastern Ontario. Established 1903 (silver discovery), incorporated 1906. Land area today ~2.07 km²; population 989 at the 2021 census.
- **North Bay** — the *Nugget*'s home since 1921, roughly 145 km (90 mi) south of Cobalt.
- **Lake Temiskaming** — long, deep lake immediately east of the Cobalt area, forming the Ontario–Quebec boundary; part of the Timiskaming Graben, a branch of the Ottawa–Bonnechere Graben. The Ottawa River flows into and out of it. Devil's Rock, a large escarpment, lies near Haileybury.
- **"Tri-Towns"** — Cobalt, Haileybury, New Liskeard (plus North Cobalt); Haileybury has been the district seat since 1912.

### 3.2 The four sighting locations

| Site | Township | Relation to Cobalt | Independent documentation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Violet Mine** | Coleman | "east of Cobalt" (1923 article) | Silver deposit discovered 1905; three shafts, deepest 928 ft; closed 1963; ~900,000 oz Ag total. Mindat / Ontario Dept. of Mines. |
| **Wettlaufer Mine** | South Lorrain | ~25 km (16 mi) SE of Cobalt; reached today from North Cobalt via Hwy 567 south ~28.5 km then ~2.3 km west | Wettlaufer Lorrain Silver Mines Ltd. 1908–1913 (250 ft shaft, 4 levels); leased 1916–19 (Comfort; Pittsburgh-Lorrain); minor leases 1935, 1937; acquired 1946 by Silanco Mining & Refining. Ontario MDI record MDI31M03NW00012. |
| **Gillies Depot / Gillies Lake** | Coleman | ~7 km (4 mi) SW of Cobalt; the railway runs along the lake about half a mile east of the Depot | T&NO section house, then station, from 1903–04; Gillies Brothers timber limit; school ~30 years; Booth Lumber barking plant upriver; federal airport built alongside during the Depression; post-WWII Silanco smelter nearby; section house physically moved to Latchford in 1965. (Peter Fancy, *Gillies Depot: A Temiskaming Journal*.) |
| **Cobalt Lode Mine** | Coleman | ~5 km (3 mi) SW of town; "near the end of the road south of Bradley Lake," rough going | Discovered 1908. Pan Silver Mines 1908–15; Adanac Silver Mines 1915–49; Cobalt Lode Silver Mines 1949–53; Cobalt Consolidated 1953–57; **Agnico Mines from 1957**. Production to end-1957: ~4.5 M oz Ag, 2.5 M lb Co, 611,000 lb Ni/Cu, 459,000 lb Cu. |

`SECONDARY INTERPRETATION` — All four sites fall inside a roughly 30 km radius centred on the Cobalt camp, and all four are **mine-adjacent or transport-adjacent**: a headframe under construction, a claim being sampled, a railway right-of-way, a mine access road. See §12 for what that does and does not imply.

`UNRESOLVED` — The 1923 article places the prospectors' claims "north and east" of the Wettlaufer Mine. One modern author (Peters) glosses this as "presumably near the southwestern shores of Lake Temiskaming." That gloss is an inference, not a source statement, and should not be promoted.

### 3.3 Two different Gillies Lakes

`SOURCE FACT` — There is a **Gillies Lake at Timmins**, roughly 164 km (100 mi) northwest. It is not the 1946/47 site. Any corpus record must disambiguate. The 1946/47 lake is the small body of water in Coleman Township on the T&NO line southwest of Cobalt.

## 4. HISTORICAL CONTEXT — THE COBALT CAMP

`SOURCE FACT`

### 4.1 Origin

The province decided in 1902 to push a railway north from North Bay — the **Temiskaming & Northern Ontario Railway (T&NO)** — partly to open farmland and partly to secure the territory. In August 1903, contractors supplying ties along the line near Mile 103, **James (J. H.) McKinley and Ernest Darragh**, noticed metal in a cut on the banks of Long Lake (later Cobalt Lake); they staked on 30 August 1903 on a J. R. Booth timber limit, and assays returned on the order of 4,000 oz of silver per ton. A second discovery is attributed to blacksmith-prospector **Fred LaRose**. Provincial geologist **Willet G. Miller** investigated and named the camp for the cobalt he found running with the silver.

### 4.2 Trajectory

- **1903–1905** — prospecting rush; T&NO service North Bay–New Liskeard from 16 January 1905; extended to Englehart 1 October 1906, Cochrane 1908.
- **1905** — ~16 mines operating in the vicinity of Cobalt.
- **1906** — Cobalt incorporated. Violet Mine (discovered 1905) under development. **This is the year of the first alleged sighting.**
- **1907** — the *Cobalt Nugget* newspaper founded; daily from 1909.
- **1908–1911** — Cobalt producing ~9% of world silver by 1908; peak 1911 at 31,507,791 oz. Population ~10,000 by 1909, possibly 12,000. Ontario's first streetcar system; a stock exchange; theatres; the Royal Exchange Building (1910). Exploration spread outward to South Lorrain, Casey Twp., Gowganda, Elk Lake, and fed later rushes at Larder Lake (1906), Porcupine/Timmins (1909), Kirkland Lake (1911), Matachewan (1916), Noranda (1920).
- **1921** — the *Nugget* moves to North Bay as the boom fades.
- **4–5 October 1922** — the **Great Fire of 1922 (Haileybury Fire)**. Small clearing fires merged under gale winds; ~650 square miles across 18 townships burned; ~43 dead; North Cobalt, Charlton, Thornloe, Heaslip destroyed; ~90% of Haileybury razed; Englehart and New Liskeard partly burned; thousands homeless. Fires had been burning in **Coleman and Lorrain townships** before the main event. **This is nine months before the 1923 blueberry-patch sighting.**
- **1920s–1930s** — silver price collapse mid-to-late 1920s; most mines closed by the 1930s; claims leased to small operators working narrow veins and "robbing pillars."
- **1950s** — intermittent revival; the **Cobalt Lode Mine produced cobalt for the U.S. government**. Agnico Mines formed 1957 and took over Cobalt Lode.
- **1960s–1980s** — Violet Mine closes 1963; most remaining operations shut through the 1970s; Silverfields and Agnico the last, into the 1980s.
- **Cumulatively** — well over 460 million oz of silver out of the district by most accounts; over 600 million oz for the wider Cobalt–Gowganda camp.

### 4.3 Why this matters to the corpus

`SECONDARY INTERPRETATION` — The four reports are spread across four utterly different economic worlds: a boomtown under construction (1906), a post-collapse, post-fire landscape worked by small lease operators (1923), a wartime-to-postwar minor revival (1946/47), and a late, contracting, bus-commuted industrial operation (1970). The human observers in each period had different reasons to be in the bush, different equipment, different light, and different expectations. **The corpus should model four different worlds, not one continuous "mining town."**

## 5. ENVIRONMENTAL FOUNDATION

`SOURCE FACT` unless marked.

### 5.1 Physical setting

Canadian Shield: Precambrian bedrock, thin acidic soils, exposed outcrop and rock cuts, glacially sculpted relief, innumerable lakes, rivers, beaver ponds and wetlands. Boreal/mixed-forest transition — spruce, balsam fir, jack pine, white and red pine, white birch, trembling aspen, with poplar and birch flushing hard after fire and logging. The Timiskaming Graben gives the lake basin its depth and the shoreline its cliffs.

### 5.2 Berries and fire

- **Lowbush blueberry (*Vaccinium angustifolium*)** and velvet-leaf blueberry (*V. myrtilloides*) are the characteristic berry of this ground. The plant is rhizomatous and fire-tolerant; rhizomes can lie dormant for long periods and sprout when light, moisture and oxygen allow. It is an aggressive coloniser of burns, clearcuts, old fields and disturbed ground — **including mine tailings** — and forms near-monoculture "barrens" in places. Fruit ripens through the summer.
- Consumers include black bear, raccoon, fox, white-tailed deer, grouse and ptarmigan.

`RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS` (flagged as inference, not source fact) — The July 1923 sighting occurred **nine months after ~650 square miles of the surrounding townships burned**. Post-fire blueberry flush is the single best-documented successional response in this vegetation type. A productive blueberry patch in the Cobalt–South Lorrain bush in the summer of 1923, and heavy bear use of it, is exactly what the ecology predicts. This strengthens the plausibility of the *scene* described in 1923 — and, neutrally, it strengthens **both** the bear reading and the "something was feeding in a good berry patch" reading. It resolves nothing about identity. It should enter the corpus as environmental grounding, clearly labelled inference.

### 5.3 Black bear ecology (relevant baseline)

- *Ursus americanus* is the region's large omnivore. Ontario adult males commonly 114–272 kg (250–600 lb); females 45–181 kg (100–400 lb). Full skeletal growth ~5 years (F), 7–8 years (M), with weight still climbing for several years after.
- Northern Ontario boreal diet shifts seasonally: green vegetation in spring → ants and insect matter in midsummer → **berries and nuts in late summer and fall**, with blueberry availability varying sharply between years (Canadian Field-Naturalist, black bear food habits, northern Ontario).
- **Colour:** the normal coat is black with a brownish muzzle and frequently a white patch at throat or chest. Other phases — brown, dark brown, **blond**, cinnamon, blue-black — occur, but Canadian Wildlife Service material states plainly that lighter phases are **more common in the west and in the mountains than in the east**. Hunting-sector sources citing check-station and research handling data from Ontario, Tennessee, Maine, New York and Minnesota report essentially no colour-phase bears east of the Mississippi. Sun-bleaching of a brown coat toward near-blond by the following summer's shed is documented. A cream or white chest "blaze" occurs across all phases.
- Lifespan: wild black bears typically live on the order of one to two decades, occasionally longer. **This matters in §12.**

### 5.4 Other wildlife the corpus can legitimately assume as present

Moose, white-tailed deer, woodland caribou (historically, north and west), grey wolf, coyote, lynx, bobcat (southern edge), fisher, marten, otter, mink, beaver, porcupine, snowshoe hare, red fox, ruffed grouse, spruce grouse, loon, raven, common raven, and — seasonally overwhelming — blackflies and mosquitoes from late May through July.

### 5.5 Seasonal frame

- **Spring (April–May):** break-up; rotten ice; flooded creeks; bare grey-brown bush; bears emerging from dens, lean, working green shoots and carrion. The 1946/47 report falls here (mid-April).
- **Summer (late June–August):** blackfly then mosquito season; long light; berry ripening; the 1923 (late July) and 1970 (early August) reports fall here.
- **Autumn (September–October):** the 1906 report falls at the front edge of this. Hyperphagia in bears; fire season historically severe (1911 Porcupine, 1916 Matheson, 1922 Haileybury).
- **Winter:** deep snow, extreme cold, bears denned; rail the reliable mover of people and freight for the first several decades.

`SECONDARY INTERPRETATION` — Three of the four reports fall in the active-bear season with abundant forage. The April report falls in the post-den, food-poor window. None falls in winter.

### 5.6 Light conditions

`RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS` — At 47.4°N in early August, sunset is around 20:45 local (EDT) and full darkness around 22:00. A bus carrying a graveyard shift with headlights on high beam is consistent with late evening or night. The 1970 record's only lighting datum is that the figure was lit by headlights. **The corpus should not assert a clock time for 1970.**

### 5.7 Industrial soundscape and disturbance

Across all four periods, the relevant bush was not quiet wilderness. It carried: steam and later diesel locomotives on the T&NO; underground and surface blasting; hoists, compressors, stamp and ball mills; drilling; timber cutting and river drives on the Montreal River; and, from the late 1920s, motor traffic on the Ferguson Highway. The Cobalt camp's peculiarity is that mining and bush interpenetrate at very small scale — headframes, dumps, shafts and trails sit directly against standing forest, and in the 1920s–1960s much of the camp was **worked-over ground reverting to brush**.

## 6. HUMAN CONTEXT BY PERIOD

`SOURCE FACT` unless marked. This is contextual grounding only — **none of it is character biography.**

### 6.1 1900–1910

Prospecting rush conditions. Men arriving from Ontario, Quebec, the United States, and — per contemporary accounts of the camp — Russia, China, Greece and Finland. Housing thrown up fast and cheap: crude wood-and-tin structures, boarding houses, hotels, tents. General store, post office, doctors, restaurants. Work was hand-and-hammer: stripping, trenching, test-pitting, hand-steel and dynamite, headframes of squared timber, horse haulage. Travel: T&NO rail (from January 1905), canoe and portage routes (the Matabanick Trail through the Gillies limit was an old fur-trade portage line), winter tote roads and sleighs, and walking. **In September 1906 there was no road network worth the name and no local daily newspaper** — the *Cobalt Nugget* did not begin until 1907.

### 6.2 1910–1930

Peak and collapse. 1911 the production high; the Great War; then silver's slide. Timber operations (Gillies Brothers; Booth Lumber's barking plant) working the Montreal River. The 1922 fire reshapes the human and physical landscape simultaneously — Haileybury rebuilt partly in retired Toronto streetcar bodies shipped north by the TTC. By 1923 the camp is a working-through-leases economy: two men "taking test samples from their claim property" is exactly the era's characteristic activity, not a company crew. Roads arrive at the end of this period: the **Ferguson Highway**, built 1925–27, North Bay to Cochrane, 420 km of gravel, muskeg and clay, officially opened 2 July 1927.

### 6.3 1930–1950

Depression; a federal airport built next to the Gillies Depot lands; wartime; then a postwar minor revival — Silanco Mining & Refining acquires the Wettlaufer property in 1946 and builds a refinery-smelter near Gillies. Gillies is a real community: school, section house, station, farms, families whose names are recorded in the local history (Amos, Cook, Berry, Cote, Leonard, Ladouceur, Funnell, Adshead, Cameron, Martin, Underwood, Oblin, Bigras). Walking the tracks into Cobalt from Gillies was ordinary transport. **The 1946/47 witnesses were doing a normal thing in a normal way.**

### 6.4 1950–1970

Cobalt Lode extracting cobalt for the U.S. government; Agnico Mines formed 1957 and consolidating properties. Highway 11 rerouted onto the Tri-Town Bypass in 1963, with **Highway 11B** created through Cobalt–North Cobalt–Haileybury–New Liskeard (Miller Ave., Cobalt St., Grandview Ave., Silver St., Lang St. through Cobalt). Mine access roads remained gravel. Shift work was bussed. Miners lived in town and commuted.

### 6.5 1970 onward

The last contraction. Most mines shut through the 1970s; Silverfields and Agnico held on into the 1980s. Cobalt became a heritage-and-nostalgia town, then a cobalt-exploration town again in the 2010s.

### 6.6 Cultural attitudes relevant to the case

`SECONDARY INTERPRETATION` — Three things about this population matter for weighing testimony, and should be held **in tension rather than resolved**:

1. **These were bush-competent people.** Prospectors, section men, and miners in 1923 and 1946 knew black bears the way a city dweller knows dogs. Three of the four accounts open with the witness saying "bear" — which is evidence both of competence *and* of an available conventional explanation being consciously rejected.
2. **Mining camps are prank cultures.** Practical jokes are a documented feature of the camp (local memoirs list them by name). The 1970 hoax reading (§14) is not an outsider's invention; per John Green it came from the *Nugget* newsroom itself.
3. **Wildman motifs are thin in the local Indigenous record.** Cree and Ojibwe traditions of the region are rich in Wendigo, cliff-dwelling little people, the Thunderbird, Mishipeshu, and the "Wild Man of the Woods"; there are giant-people cycles (*mistapewak*; the Hairy Hearts). But `SECONDARY INTERPRETATION` — as Peters himself notes — legendary wildmen are **not** prominent elements of Cree/Ojibwe oral tradition in this specific area, and **no source connects Old Yellow Top to any named Indigenous tradition.** Any claim that Old Yellow Top "comes from First Nations legend" is `DO NOT PROMOTE` (see §16).
4. **The 1923 report predates the Sasquatch publicity wave.** J. W. Burns's *Maclean's* article that popularised "Sasquatch" appeared in April 1929. This is a genuine point in the 1923 report's favour on the question of cultural contamination — the 1923 witnesses had no national Bigfoot template to pattern-match against. It does **not** mean they had no template at all: "wild man of the woods" motifs were widely available in settler folklore.

#### 7.1.1 The 1906 source-trail problem, stated plainly

`SOURCE FACT` — **The 1906 event has no known contemporaneous documentation of any kind.** It enters the record as one retrospective clause inside a 1923 newspaper item. Its evidentiary status is therefore: *a 1923 newspaper asserted that a report had been made in 1906.*

`DO NOT PROMOTE` — The widely copied account that the creature was seen **repeatedly throughout the construction of the Violet Mine**, "in the distance as well as relatively close to the work area," never angry or territorial, apparently curious, watching the men work from a safe distance (Pine Barrens Institute, 2018, and derivatives) is **flatly contradicted** by the 1923 text, which says there was one report and that the creature had not been seen since. It is an L4 embellishment overwriting an L2 source. It must be quarantined.

`UNKNOWN` — What "ape-like" meant to a 1923 Northern Ontario reporter. The word is the *newspaper's*, not a witness's. In 1923 the available referents were zoo and circus apes, illustrated magazines, and missing-link discourse. It signals "not a bear, upright, hairy, man-shaped" more than it signals any specific anatomy. **Do not infer brow ridges, sagittal crest, arm length, facial structure, or anything else from this one adjective.**

#### 7.2.1 What this article establishes about naming

`SOURCE FACT` — Three naming facts come from this one item, and they are among the most secure things in the case:
1. "Old Yellow-Top" was **already a nickname in use** by July 1923.
2. The nickname is explained **by a light-coloured mane**, not by a yellow crown patch, not by yellow eyes, not by anything else.
3. "Pre-Cambrian Shield Man" is **newspaper headline language** of the same date.

`CONTESTED` — "mane" (newspaper framing) versus "head was kind of yellow" (witness paraphrase) are not the same anatomical claim. A mane implies hair around neck and shoulders; a yellow head implies the crown/face region. **The 1970 report reinforces "mane" (light hair down toward the shoulders); the 1946/47 report reinforces "head" (a light head). Both readings are live. Do not average them.**

#### 7.4.1 Voice separation — mandatory

The 1970 record must be held as **five separate voices**, never collapsed:

1. **WHAT THE DRIVER CLAIMED** — dark form crossing; thought bear; it turned into the lights; light hair down toward the shoulders; not a bear; he had heard of the thing before without believing it; uncertainty about continuing to drive.
2. **WHAT THE PASSENGER CLAIMED** — glimpsed it from the front of the bus; looked like a bear at first; didn't walk like one; stooped; possibly a wounded bear; **he did not believe in the creature**; his father used to talk about it.
3. **WHAT THE NEWSPAPER REPORTED** — 27 miners; graveyard shift; the bus out of control; near-plunge down a rock cut; no injuries; the "Pre-Cambrian Shield Man" label; a count of appearances that is internally wrong; and (per Peters) a closing quip suggesting the figure might have been someone leaving a local drinking establishment.

5. **WHAT INVESTIGATORS CLAIMED ABOUT THE NEWSPAPER'S ATTITUDE** — Green's inference from the quip, plus Davis's reported newsroom inquiry. **This is a claim about journalists' private opinions, sourced to a researcher's recollection of an associate's inquiry. `CONTESTED` — Note the asymmetry that makes this event so hard to read: the **driver** moved from "bear" to "not a bear," while the **passenger** moved from "bear" to "maybe a wounded bear" and stated he didn't believe the legend anyway. The two published witnesses do not corroborate each other's conclusion. They corroborate only that **something crossed the road and that both initially took it for a bear.**

## 11. COMPETING EXPLANATIONS

Each explanation is stated in the form its proponents give it, with what supports it, what cuts against it, and what remains open. **No explanation is endorsed here.**

### 11.1 American black bear (the primary naturalistic reading)

**SUPPORTING EVIDENCE**
- Three of four accounts have the witness identify a bear *first*, unprompted: 1923 ("what looked like a bear"), 1970 driver ("at first I thought it was a big bear"), 1970 passenger ("looked like a bear to me at first").
- The 1923 setting is textbook bear: a blueberry patch in late July in northern Ontario boreal forest, in the season when *Vaccinium* dominates black bear diet, in a district that had burned the previous autumn.
- Black bears stand bipedally to see and smell, and will rise when startled. Rising in response to a thrown stone is normal.
- The 1970 passenger's "stooped over" describes a bear's carriage better than a hominid's.
- Standing bears are the single most commonly cited naturalistic source of Bigfoot reports generally.
- Black bear coat colour is genuinely variable: brown, dark brown, cinnamon, blond, blue-black phases exist within the species, and sun-bleaching can lighten a brown coat toward near-blond by the following summer's shed. A cream-to-white chest blaze occurs across phases.
- Joe Nickell, in the *Skeptical Inquirer*, explicitly assigns the Cobalt case to a bear, describing the figure as "a blond-maned black bear," and places it in a series of regional Bigfoot variants he reads as bears (Skunk Ape, Traverspine "Gorilla," Fouke Monster, Momo, Big Muddy, the Sister Lakes creature).
- The absence of any physical trace across 64 years is exactly what a misidentification hypothesis predicts.

**CONTRADICTING EVIDENCE / DIFFICULTIES**
- **Colour-phase bears are rare in eastern Canada.** Canadian Wildlife Service material states lighter phases are more common in the west and in the mountains; hunting-sector compilations of Ontario, Maine, New York, Minnesota and Tennessee check-station and research data report essentially no colour-phase bears east of the Mississippi. A blond-headed black bear in Temiskaming is therefore **not an ordinary bear either** — it is an unusual bear. The bear hypothesis explains the shape but must still explain the colour.
- Wilson in 1923 was a prospector who worked in that bush and said explicitly that it was unlike any bear he had seen — a competence-based rejection, not an ignorant one.
- Latreille in 1970 reached "it couldn't have been a bear" *after* getting a headlit look, not before.
- A bear that crosses in front of a bus and turns into high beams without bolting is behaviourally unremarkable, but the driver's stated reason for rejecting bear is the **hair length to the shoulders**, which a bear does not obviously present.

**UNRESOLVED**
- Whether an anomalous light-crowned or sun-bleached bear was known in the Cobalt district in any of these years. This is potentially checkable in the *Nugget*, in Ontario Department of Lands and Forests records, and in local hunting lore. **Nobody appears to have checked.**
- Whether the "mane"/"light hair to the shoulders" description is compatible with a bear's ruff, a wet or matted coat under headlights, or a mange-affected or injured animal (Cormack's own "maybe it was a wounded bear").

**SOURCE** — Nickell, *Skeptical Inquirer* (2015), citing Green 1978 pp. 248–249. L3.

### 11.2 Injured, diseased, or anomalously coloured bear

**SUPPORTING** — Raised by a witness himself: Cormack floated a wounded bear in 1970. Injury or mange alters gait, posture and coat appearance, and can produce a stooped, awkward, "wrong-walking" animal. Mange and hair loss can also produce patchy light/dark contrast.
**CONTRADICTING** — Does not explain a *repeating* light-headed motif across 1923, 1946/47 and 1970 unless one accepts either coincidence or a local population with the trait.
**UNRESOLVED** — Never investigated at the time.
**SOURCE** — Witness statement (1970), L2.

### 11.3 A person

**SUPPORTING** — The *Nugget*'s own closing quip in 1970 (per Peters/Green) gestured at a patron leaving a local drinking establishment. Dark clothing plus light hair reads directly as "dark body, light head." Mining camps had transient populations, bush workers, trappers, and men walking roads at night. In 1946/47, "walked very much like a man" is trivially explained if it was a man.
**CONTRADICTING** — Does not fit the 1923 feeding-in-a-berry-patch scene well, nor the reaction to a thrown stone. Does not explain 1906 (though nothing explains 1906).
**UNRESOLVED** — No one appears to have canvassed for a person in any of the four cases.
**SOURCE** — Newspaper quip (L2 via L3); general.

### 11.4 Prank / hoax, specifically for 1970

**SUPPORTING** — Per Green, via Peters: the *Nugget*'s closing joke indicated the paper's own attitude, and Green's associate **Bill Davis** inquired at the *Nugget* office and was told the journalists regarded the story as a miners' prank. Mining camps have documented prank cultures. A costumed figure crossing a known bus route at a known time is a low-effort, high-payoff joke on a busload of workmates — and a legend with a name and a 64-year pedigree is exactly the kind of thing a prank references.
**CONTRADICTING** — A prank that nearly put a bus over a rock cut with 27 men aboard is a serious matter; nobody appears to have been identified, confessed, or been accused. Cormack, a professed non-believer seated at the front, was not in on it and did not call it a prank. The prank reading also does not touch 1906, 1923 or 1946/47.
**UNRESOLVED** — The Davis inquiry is undocumented outside Green's account. The *Nugget* staff's actual view is **a third-hand claim about private opinion**.
**SOURCE** — Green, via Peters 2024. L3. **Note: the bigfootencounters transcription of the 1970 article does not contain the closing quip at all.** Either the transcription is abridged or the quip is being remembered from a different edition. `UNRESOLVED`.

### 11.5 Unknown hominid / "Marked Hominid" class

**SUPPORTING** — Coleman & Huyghe, *The Field Guide to Bigfoot, Yeti, and Other Mystery Primates Worldwide* (1999), place Old Yellow Top in a class they call **Marked Hominid**, defined by two-toned or multicoloured pelage. The label carries a double etymology: Mark A. Hall, the cryptozoologist who first separated out the type (he called them "Taller Hominids"), and the Mansi word *Mecheny* — "Marked One" — a Western Siberian wildman reported with an anomalous white patch on one forearm. Class traits as they describe them: subarctic range, roughly seven-foot stature, two-toned coat. They note the parallel with the **Traverspine "Gorilla"** of Labrador, reported with a shock of white hair on the crown.
**CONTRADICTING** — The class is a **construct built out of the reports it then explains**. The seven-foot stature attributed to Old Yellow Top by way of this class **does not come from any Cobalt source** — it is a class attribute assigned to the case and then repeated back as though it were a Cobalt datum. This is the single clearest instance of circular provenance in the whole file (see §16).
**UNRESOLVED** — Everything. There is no type specimen, no physical evidence, no accepted taxon.
**SOURCE** — Coleman & Huyghe 1999. L3.

### 11.6 Folklore amplification / composite legend

**SUPPORTING** — A named local legend existed by 1923 at the latest and was in family conversation by 1970 (Cormack's father). A named legend supplies a template: once "Old Yellow Top" exists, any dark animal with a light patch becomes a candidate. The *Nugget* had an institutional memory of the story and reached for it in its own headlines across nearly fifty years. The 1970 headline even miscounts the appearances, which is what a newsroom does when working from its own morgue rather than from records.
**CONTRADICTING** — Does not account for the 1923 report, which is the one made by people with no obvious incentive and before the Sasquatch template existed nationally. And it does not explain why the *specific* trait repeated is a light head rather than something more generic.
**UNRESOLVED** — Whether the name circulated between 1906 and 1923, and how.
**SOURCE** — Structural inference from the sources themselves. L3.

### 11.7 Multiple unrelated animals / multiple unrelated events

**SUPPORTING** — The four reports are 17, 23–24, and 24 years apart, in four locations, with no continuity of witness, and with descriptions that differ in the details that matter. Treating them as four independent events is the **null hypothesis** and no source has ever tested against it.
**CONTRADICTING** — Nothing, except the narrative convenience of a single creature.
**SOURCE** — Structural.

### 11.8 Explanations deliberately NOT included

Per the research discipline of this project, no explanation has been invented for completeness. Specifically excluded for lack of any identifiable basis in the sources: escaped exotic primate; feral human; relict *Gigantopithecus*; paranormal or interdimensional readings; any Indigenous-tradition-derived identification. **None of these has ever been proposed by a traceable source in connection with Cobalt.**

## 12. CONTINUITY ANALYSIS — COULD THESE BE ONE INDIVIDUAL?

**This section constructs the problem. It does not answer it.**

### 12.1 What supports continuity

1. **A distinctive and unusual shared trait.** A dark body with a light head or mane is specific. It is not the generic Bigfoot description. It recurs in 1923, 1946/47 and 1970 (the 1906 trait is retro-attributed).
2. **Tight geography.** All four sites lie within roughly 30 km of Cobalt, and three within about 10 km of it. That is well within the home range of a large mammal.
3. **Consistent size class by implication.** Nothing in any account suggests a small animal; all suggest something notable enough to be reported.
4. **Consistent behavioural posture.** Non-aggressive, withdrawing, no escalation — across all four.
5. **Witness independence.** The 1923 prospectors, the 1946/47 woman, and the 1970 bus passengers had no evident connection to one another and, for 1923, no national Bigfoot template.
6. **Habitat continuity.** The same forest, the same berry ground, the same corridors of human transport, across the whole period.

### 12.2 What weakens continuity

1. **64 years.** The span from September 1906 to August 1970. For comparison, wild black bears typically live on the order of one to two decades. **No North American mammal of the implied size class routinely persists as a recognisable individual for 64 years.** To keep one individual, the corpus must either posit an extraordinary lifespan or silently smuggle one in. `DO NOT` do that.
2. **The trait is not distinctive enough to be an identification.** "Dark with a light head" would fit several individuals of a population, several bears with a shared genetic trait, or several unrelated misidentifications.
3. **The descriptions are not actually the same.** 1923: yellow *head*, black body, ran. 1946/47: dark with a light *head*, ambling, man-like walk. 1970: dark with light hair down to the *shoulders*, stooped, not walking like a bear. The light region migrates; the gait flips between man-like and stooped.
4. **The 1906 description does not exist.** Continuity across four events is really continuity across three, plus an undescribed anchor.
5. **The name itself creates continuity.** Once a district has a named creature, every subsequent sighting is filed under the name — by witnesses, by the newspaper, and by researchers. The 1970 *Nugget* headline explicitly continues a series. **The unity of the case may be an artefact of the nickname rather than evidence of an animal.**
6. **"Old" is doing invisible work.** The affectionate intensifier in "Old Yellow Top" smuggles in an aged, singular, persisting individual. No source says the animal was old.
7. **Gaps of 17, 23 and 24 years with no intervening reports** are hard to reconcile with a resident individual in a district full of prospectors, trappers, section men and hunters.

### 12.3 What cannot be determined

- Whether any two of the four events involved the same animal.
- Whether the light-headed trait was heritable, individual, pathological, or artefactual.
- Whether anything at all was present in 1906.
- Whether the gaps represent absence of the animal or absence of reporting.

`UNRESOLVED` — **The continuity question is not answerable from the record and must be carried into the interview as an open question rather than a premise.** Note carefully: if the interview engine ever produces a first-person voice that remembers all four events, that memory is a **character construction**, not a historical claim, and must be tagged as such at generation time.

## 13. THE BEAR QUESTION — DEDICATED TREATMENT

Per the research brief, this is treated separately and without a verdict. §11.1 gives the case. This section isolates the **human perceptual factors**, which are where the bear question actually lives.

### 13.1 Factors favouring misidentification in each event

| Event | Perceptual factors present |
|---|---|
| **1906** | 17-year gap between event and any documentation; complete loss of original testimony; transmission through camp talk; no description to test. |
| **1923** | Distance unrecorded. Animal initially seen from behind, feeding, i.e. in its least diagnostic posture. Colour judgement at distance in daylight. Surprise, then a provoked reaction, then rapid departure — total observation time likely seconds. |
| **1946/47** | Witness stated **she did not get a clear look.** Mid-April: bare grey-brown bush, possible residual snow, high-contrast lighting. Moving observer on uneven ground. |
| **1970** | Night. Headlights. A moving vehicle. A few seconds. Headlight illumination flattens depth, exaggerates size, and washes colour toward light at the reflective edges — the exact conditions under which a bear's guard hairs or a wet ruff could read as "light hair." Then an emergency braking event that would encode the memory under high arousal. |

### 13.2 Factors resisting a simple misidentification reading

- Three of four witnesses **considered and rejected** bear. Witness rejection is not proof, but it is not nothing: these were people for whom bears were common.
- The rejections were made on **different grounds** each time: colour (1923), gait (1946/47), hair length (1970 driver), gait again (1970 passenger). Convergence on "not a bear" from divergent reasons is harder to explain as a single shared error than convergence from a single shared cue.
- The specific recurring anomaly is a **colour pattern**, not a shape. Shape errors are the classic bear misidentification; colour is a different kind of claim.

### 13.3 Cultural contamination and memory reconstruction

`SECONDARY INTERPRETATION` — By 1946 and certainly by 1970, "Old Yellow Top" was an available local category with a *name* and a *defining feature*. A witness who sees an ambiguous dark animal in a district with a famous light-headed monster is primed to look for the light head. The 1970 passenger's father talked about it; the newspaper filed it under the existing headline. **Only the 1923 report is meaningfully free of this priming** — and even it uses a nickname that already existed.

`UNRESOLVED` — Whether the 1923 nickname's pre-existence means there was 17 years of local sighting tradition that went unrecorded, or 17 years of a single anecdote hardening into a name.

## 15. RESEARCH GAPS

### 15.1 Never done, and cheap to do

1. **Pull the actual *Nugget* pages.** North Bay *Nugget* runs 1909–2014 and is searchable on Newspapers.com (713,000+ pages); the Cobalt/North Bay *Nugget* is also partially indexed by the Nipissing District Branch of the OGS, and the Nipissing–Parry Sound library archive holds *Nugget* material. **Target dates: 27 July 1923; 16 April 1946 AND 16 April 1947; 5 August 1970.** This single action resolves C-1 through C-9 and C-18.
2. **Search 1906–1907 papers for any contemporaneous Violet Mine item.** The *Cobalt Nugget* did not exist until 1907, but the **Haileyburian** (from 1904) and the **New Liskeard Speaker** (from 1906) did, and both are digitised in part; the *Haileyburian* and *Cobalt Weekly Post* were later merged into the *Temiskaming Speaker*, whose archive was digitised in 2003. Mining trade press (*Northern Miner*, *Canadian Mining Journal*, Montreal and Toronto dailies covering the camp) is also worth a pass.
3. **Check whether the *Nugget* ran follow-ups** in the weeks after each item — especially a search-party story in April 1946/47 and any letters or corrections in August 1970.

### 15.2 Harder, but decisive

4. **Claim records for MacAuley and Wilson**, South Lorrain Township, 1920s — Ontario mining recorder's records, assessment work filings, the Ontario Department of Mines annual reports. A claim number would anchor the 1923 location to the ground.
5. **Identify the 1946/47 witnesses.** Gillies Depot was tiny and its families are enumerated in Peter Fancy's *Gillies Depot: A Temiskaming Journal* (with 177 photographs and an index). Cross-referencing "woman with a son, resident near Gillies Depot, April 1946/47" against that index is feasible. **Ethical note: she asked not to be named. Identification for research is defensible; publication of her name is a separate decision.**
6. **Cobalt Lode Mine / Agnico records for August 1970** — a bus leaving the road with 27 men aboard is the kind of thing that generates a company incident report, an insurance claim, or an OPP occurrence. None has been sought.
7. **Trace Bill Davis's inquiry.** Green's papers and correspondence, and Dahinden's, are the place to look for the chain that produced the clippings in the first place.

### 15.3 Probably unrecoverable

8. The identities of the 1906 Violet Mine crew.
9. What the 1906 men actually described.
10. Whether "Yellow Top" was in use before 1923.
11. Interviews with any of the other ~25 passengers in 1970.

## 19. THE CHARACTER BOUNDARY — LAYER A AND LAYER B

### 19.1 Statement of boundary (mandatory preamble for any downstream character system)

> The historical corpus for Old Yellow Top establishes **a human-recorded case**: that people in the Cobalt district of Ontario reported, in print, on four occasions between 1923 and 1970, a dark hair-covered figure with a lighter head or mane.
>
> It does **not** establish a biological species.
> It does **not** establish that all sightings involved one individual.
> It does **not** establish a predetermined biography.
> It does **not** establish family relationships.
> It does **not** establish motive.
> It does **not** establish intelligence level.
> It does **not** establish lifespan.
> It does **not** establish personal memory.
> It does **not** establish sex, age, height, weight, odour, voice, diet, range, or habits beyond one reported growl and one reported meal of blueberries.
>
> All of the above are candidates for **character reconstruction only**, and must be generated as such, marked as such, and never written back into the historical layer.

### 19.2 LAYER A — WORLD KNOWLEDGE (canonical; the corpus may assert these)

**Places and things that existed:**
- Cobalt, Ontario existed; founded on the 1903 silver discoveries; incorporated 1906; a boomtown of roughly ten thousand people by 1909.
- The Violet Mine existed in Coleman Township; discovered 1905; a headframe was built there; three shafts; closed 1963.
- The Wettlaufer Mine existed in South Lorrain Township; worked 1908–1913 and intermittently afterward by lessees; acquired by Silanco in 1946.
- Gillies Depot existed: a T&NO section house and station settlement on the old Matabanick portage route, with a school, timber operations, and, later, an airport and a smelter. Gillies Lake lies about half a mile east of it, with the railway running along its shore.
- The Cobalt Lode Mine existed; discovered 1908; owned by Agnico Mines from 1957; reached by a rough gravel road southwest of town.
- The Temiskaming & Northern Ontario Railway existed and carried people and freight through all four periods. The Ferguson Highway opened 2 July 1927. Highway 11B ran through Cobalt from 1963.
- The Great Fire of 1922 burned roughly 650 square miles across 18 townships on 4–5 October 1922 and killed about 43 people.
- Black bears, moose, wolves, blackflies, and lowbush blueberries are and were present.

**Documents that exist:**
- The North Bay *Nugget* published an item on 27 July 1923 reporting two prospectors' encounter and naming the creature.
- The *Nugget* published an item on 16 April (1946 or 1947) reporting a woman and her son's sighting near Gillies Lake.
- The *Nugget* published an item on 5 August 1970 reporting the bus incident near the Cobalt Lode Mine.
- The 1923 item asserted, retrospectively, that a first report had been made in September 1906 by men building the Violet Mine headframe.

**Human statements that were made:**
- A prospector named Wilson said the animal's head was a yellowish colour and its body black like a bear's, and that it was unlike any bear he knew.
- A woman near Gillies Lake said she saw a dark hairy animal with a light head walk off the tracks into the bush, and that she did not get a clear look.
- A bus driver named Latreille said he first thought it was a big bear, then saw light hair almost down to its shoulders when it turned into his headlights, and concluded it could not have been a bear.
- A miner named Larry Cormack said it looked like a bear at first but didn't walk like one, that it was stooped over, and that it might have been a wounded bear; he also said his father used to talk about the creature.

**Names in use:**
- "Old Yellow-Top" was in local newspaper use by July 1923, explained as deriving from a light-coloured mane.
- "Pre-Cambrian Shield Man" was used as a *Nugget* headline label in 1923 and 1970.

### 19.3 LAYER B — CHARACTER KNOWLEDGE (`CHARACTER DISCOVERY REQUIRED`; the corpus must NOT fill these)

`OPEN CHARACTER RECONSTRUCTION` — every item below:

- Birth; parents; siblings; kin of any kind; mate; offspring; lineage.
- Age at any point; lifespan; whether it ages.
- Any name it has for itself; whether it has the concept of a name.
- Whether it knows the name "Yellow Top," and if so what it makes of it.
- Den, shelter, home, sleeping place; whether it has one.
- Territory, range, routes, boundaries; seasonal movement.
- Diet beyond the single blueberry-patch observation.
- Whether it caches, hunts, scavenges, fishes, or forages exclusively.
- Communication: whether it vocalises beyond the growl; whether it communicates with others of its kind; whether it has language.
- Intelligence: reasoning, planning, memory, time-sense, self-awareness.
- Tool use.
- Social structure; whether there are others; whether it is alone.
- Relationships with other animals, including bears.
- Its relationship to humans: fear, indifference, curiosity, resentment, protectiveness, dependence.
- Whether it was aware of being observed on any of the four occasions.
- Whether it remembers specific people — Wilson, the woman on the tracks, the driver.
- Whether the 1906, 1923, 1946/47 and 1970 observations were the same individual.
- Its motives for being near mines, tracks and roads.
- Its response to the thrown stone beyond the growl: pain, anger, surprise, fear, contempt.
- Whether it has a personal history spanning those decades.
- Its interior sensory world: what it smells, hears, sees; how machines register to it.
- Any emotional, moral, or spiritual dimension.
- Why it would consent to being interviewed at all.

### 19.4 The firewall rule

Any downstream generation must tag every produced statement with its layer. **A Layer B answer, once generated, must never be promoted to Layer A, cited as evidence, or used to resolve a Layer A contradiction.** If the interview engine produces a memory of 1923 that "settles" whether the creature ran on two legs, that memory is fiction; C-10 remains unresolved.

## 20. INTERVIEW DISCOVERY MAP

This is a **map of gaps**, not a questionnaire. Each node names a region of ignorance and the historical hook that opens onto it. The engine should discover; it should not assume. Where a node has a hook in the record, the hook is given — that is the only thing the interview may take as given.

**ORIGIN** — *Hook: none.* Nothing in the record touches beginnings. Open in full: where from, how long, whether there was ever a first.
**CHILDHOOD** — *Hook: none.* Whether the concept applies at all is itself a discovery.
**FAMILY** — *Hook: none.* Whether "family" is a category it possesses.
**AGE** — *Hook: the nickname's "Old," which is not a report.* Whether it has a sense of its own duration.
**PHYSICAL SELF** — *Hooks: dark body; lighter head or mane; capable of standing; a growl.* Everything else — hands, feet, face, height, weight, smell — is discovery. Ask what it notices about itself, not what it looks like.
**HOME** — *Hook: none.* Whether shelter exists; whether a place is "home."
**TERRITORY** — *Hook: a ~30 km neighbourhood around Cobalt across 64 years.* Whether that reflects range, or only where people happened to be looking.
**FOOD** — *Hook: blueberries, late July 1923.* Everything else open. What the burn of 1922 did to the ground it feeds on.
**SEASONS** — *Hooks: September, July, April, August.* Winter is entirely absent from the record. **What happens in winter is the single largest ecological gap.**
**MOVEMENT** — *Hooks: standing, ambling, walking, crossing, running, stooping.* Whether upright travel is normal or occasional.
**SENSORY WORLD** — *Hook: it turned toward headlights.* What light does to it. What it hears before it sees.
**COMMUNICATION** — *Hook: one growl, directed at two men who had thrown a stone.* Whether that was speech, warning, pain, or reflex.
**INTELLIGENCE** — *Hook: none.* Approach obliquely: what it understands about mines, trains, roads.
**TOOLS** — *Hook: none.* Note the asymmetry worth exploring: a human threw a stone at it; nothing is recorded of it using anything.
**HUMANS** — *Hooks: four encounters, none initiated by it, none escalated by it.* Whether humans are weather, threat, curiosity, or kin.
**MINERS** — *Hooks: headframe crew, prospectors, a busload of graveyard-shift men.* All four encounters involve people going to or from mine work.
**RAILWAYS** — *Hook: the T&NO line at Gillies Lake; a figure moving off the tracks into the bush.* What a right-of-way is to it — corridor, hazard, boundary.
**ROADS** — *Hook: a gravel mine road at night; a crossing completed while a bus went out of control.* Whether roads are crossed deliberately or endured.
**MACHINERY** — *Hooks: headframes, hoists, locomotives, a bus.* What the noise means.
**FIRE** — *Hook: the Great Fire of 1922 burned this ground nine months before the 1923 sighting; the region also burned in 1911 and 1916.* **This is the richest unexplored hook in the entire record** — a documented, dateable catastrophe that reshaped the exact landscape, and about which the sources are entirely silent.
**WEATHER** — *Hooks: mid-April break-up; late-July heat; early-August night.* Cold, snow, ice, blackfly season.
**OTHER ANIMALS** — *Hook: the bear it was repeatedly mistaken for.* What it makes of being confused with a bear, if anything.
**SAFETY** — *Hook: it withdrew every time.* Whether withdrawal is policy, temperament, or fear.
**FEAR** — *Hook: a stone was thrown at it and it stood up and growled.* What it was doing in that second.
**CURIOSITY** — *Hook: it turned to face the headlights instead of running.* The only recorded instance of it orienting toward humans rather than away.
**CONFLICT** — *Hook: one thrown stone; one near-fatal bus accident it caused without touching anyone.* Whether it understood either.
**MEMORY** — *Hook: none.* Whether it holds events across years at all.
**IDENTITY** — *Hook: none.* Whether "I" is available to it.
**NAMES** — *Hooks: "Old Yellow-Top" (1923), "Pre-Cambrian Shield Man" (1923, 1970), "the half-man, half-beast" (1946/47).* Whether it knows it has been named, and by whom, and what naming is.
**LEGEND** — *Hook: by 1970 a miner's father talked about it at home; the newspaper had a house label for it.* It existed as a story in that district for at least 47 years. Whether it knows it is a story.
**THE FOUR SIGHTINGS:**
 - **1906** — *The blank.* A headframe going up east of town, September, unnamed men, no description. The interview may discover anything here **only because nothing is documented.** That makes it the most dangerous node: whatever is said cannot be checked, so it must be flagged most heavily.
 - **1923** — *The richest.* A berry patch on burned-over ground, two men sampling claims, a thrown stone, a standing, a growl, a departure. Open: distance, awareness, intent, what the stone felt like, whether it had seen the men before.
 - **1946/47** — *The quietest.* A woman and a boy walking the rails; an unhurried move off the tracks; no acknowledgement recorded in either direction. Open: whether it saw them, whether it cared, why it was on the line.
 - **1970** — *The loudest.* Headlights, a bus, a crossing completed, a vehicle nearly off a rock cut, twenty-seven men aboard, no injuries. Open: whether it registered the bus as danger, whether it knew what nearly happened.
**MODERN WORLD** — *Hooks: the mines closed; Cobalt shrank to under a thousand people; the bush took back the workings.* No sighting has been reported since 1970. The silence of 56 years is itself a question.
**REASON FOR PARTICIPATING IN THE INTERVIEW** — *Hook: none whatsoever.* This is a pure construction and should be treated as the most conspicuously fictional element of the whole exercise. It is also the node that determines the voice of everything else, so it should be settled **explicitly and visibly**, not smuggled in.

### 20.1 Interview cautions

- **Never ask a question that presupposes a Layer B fact.** "How old are you?" assumes age is a category it has. Better: "Do you count anything?"
- **Do not ask it to adjudicate the record.** Asking "were all four of those you?" invites the character to resolve C-20, which the character has no authority to do.
- **Do not let it supply physical description it was never given.** If it describes its own hands, that is fiction; tag it.
- **The name question is the sharpest tool available.** It was named by others, in print, in a language it has no recorded access to, for a feature on its own head. Everything about the case's epistemics is contained in that.

## 23. FINAL SOURCE-QUALITY ASSESSMENT

### 23.1 Grade of the case

| Dimension | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Documentation that reports were made** | **Good.** Three dated newspaper items, two with named witnesses, from a real regional daily with a continuous run — verifiable in a commercial archive by anyone willing to look. |
| **Documentation of what was seen** | **Poor.** Total surviving descriptive content across 64 years is a few dozen words. No distances, no durations, no times of day, no measurements. |
| **Independence of witnesses** | **Good, within events.** Four unconnected sets of witnesses across 64 years. Weakened by the fact that a named legend existed throughout and shaped what got reported and how. |
| **Physical evidence** | **Zero.** No tracks, casts, hair, scat, photographs, film, or specimens — not merely unconvincing evidence, but none at all, and none claimed in the source items. |
| **Contemporaneity** | **Three of four events are documented within days. One (1906) is documented seventeen years late and secondhand.** |
| **Provenance chain to the primary documents** | **Weak.** A single channel: Dahinden → Green → one web transcription → everything else. The transcription is demonstrably imperfect. |
| **Contamination by later accretion** | **Severe.** Height, weight, odour, eyes, footprints, a limp, a photograph, a First Nations origin, a full narrative rendering of the 1946/47 event — all added after the fact and all now dominant online. |
| **Quality of skeptical treatment** | **Thin.** One brief *Skeptical Inquirer* item. Nobody has tested the bear reading against the eastern colour-phase rarity problem, and nobody has done archival work on the naturalistic side either. |
| **Quality of advocacy treatment** | **Thin but honest at the top.** Green preserved the clippings and, notably, also preserved the *Nugget*'s dismissive attitude toward the 1970 story rather than suppressing it — which is a point in his favour as a compiler. |
| **Overall** | **A small, well-dated, badly-described, physically evidence-free newspaper case, of real interest for its 1923 pre-dating of the Sasquatch publicity wave, and now buried under six decades of accretion.** |

### 23.2 The three things most worth carrying forward

1. **The 1923 item is the spine.** It names the creature, explains the name, supplies the only quoted testimony of substance, supplies the only vocalisation, and establishes the 1906 claim. If any one document in this case deserves to be found on microfilm, it is this one.
2. **Every physical detail that makes Old Yellow Top vivid online is an addition.** Seven feet, four hundred pounds, glowing eyes, a stench, footprints, a limp, a photograph. Strip them and what remains is a dark animal with a pale head, seen briefly, four times, by people who mostly said "bear" first.
3. **The name may be the actual subject of this case.** "Old Yellow-Top" existed in print by 1923 and organised every subsequent report — the newspaper filed under it, a miner's father talked about it, researchers catalogued under it, and the web has spent thirty years decorating it. A study of the name is fully supportable by the evidence. A study of the animal is not.

### 23.3 Closing statement of discipline

This document does not decide whether Old Yellow Top existed. It records what humans documented, what witnesses reported, what later researchers claimed, what is reasonable inference, what is contested, what is unknown, and what belongs to the character interview.

A first-person Old Yellow Top can be built on this foundation. It should be built **in the space the record leaves empty**, not on top of the record — and the seams should stay visible.
